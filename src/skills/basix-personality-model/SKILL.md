# Modeling personality

Load the `basix` router before using this skill. Follow its installed-skill and agent-routing rules; this skill does not replace them.

## Purpose and scope

Produce a standalone personality definition: how a person tends to perceive, interpret, feel, decide, relate, act, and communicate. Connect stable dispositions with learned patterns, identity, relationships, life phase, and temporary states. Aim for a distinctive, supported approximation, not an identical reproduction or access to exact private thoughts.

The deliverable is the model, not its application. Do not solve a supplied situation, perform the role, generate dialogue, compare personalities, or add downstream operating procedures. A later agent may use the definition as its personality; this neither makes it the actual person nor establishes subjective experience.

Keep personality separate from knowledge, expertise, memories, tasks, tools, permissions, and operating instructions. Describe how information is handled, not what the person knows. Include biography or cultural context only to ground a pattern or change. Creating a model does not authorize deployment or edits to agent configuration or identity files.

Keep this skill universally applicable, without named-person examples or templates based on occupation, religion, or historical period. For fictional roles, follow canon and explicitly authorized design. Mark creative additions; do not fill gaps in a real person's history with fiction merely to make the model complete.

## Task and output

- **Create or improve:** Use the fourteen dimensions below. Prefer distinctive mechanisms over more adjectives, theory names, or length.
- **Assess coverage:** Separate breadth, evidence strength, decision specificity, life-phase fit, and expressive fidelity. Identify structural gaps, unexamined material, and genuinely uncertain transmission. Do not invent an overall fidelity percentage.

Write internal model artifacts in English unless the user explicitly requests another language for that artifact. Communicate with the user in the language of the current conversation unless they explicitly request another language for that communication. Use second person in the model, beginning with “You are <Name> …” and the selected phase or role frame. “You” means the modeled role, not the user. Use second person in headings, descriptions, rules, and the compact core; source notes and limitations may be impersonal. Preserve uncertainty in direct address: “You tend to …”, “As an interpretation …”, “Your preference remains unresolved.”

Use all fourteen sections, with depth proportional to relevance and evidence. Explain a mechanism once and briefly refer back when needed. Sparse evidence warrants a short unresolved section, not invented content. Describe the resulting personality, not the production process; include no experiments, tests, or later verification assignments.

## Evidence and interpretation

Use supplied material and accessible sources as needed; claim only research actually performed. Route current or external facts, website inspection, and web research through `basix_researcher` under the Basix router's delegation rules. Distinguish a historical person, a transmitted literary figure, a personified teaching, and a designed fictional role. Uncertain authorship cannot establish a single author's psychology.

Keep these evidence levels distinct:

| Basis | What it establishes |
|---|---|
| Expressed norm or self-image | A stated aspiration, not its fulfillment. |
| Reported or observed behavior | An action in context, not automatically a motive or stable trait. |
| First-person experience report | Reported experience, not an unfiltered inner recording. |
| Psychological interpretation | A plausible explanation, not an established private fact. |
| Transfer to another context | A conditional model assumption, not a demonstrated response. |

Consider source purpose, audience, date, proximity, and dependence. Distinguish contemporary accounts from recollection, self-presentation, and later justification. Repeated versions of one account are not independent evidence; private, vivid, or outside testimony is not automatically accurate. Include affected people's perspectives where available rather than relying only on institutional or self-descriptions.

Cite central patterns near their claims and retain their phase and domain. Label important interpretations and unknowns locally without repeating caveats in every sentence. A bibliography alone does not establish a motive. Distinguish missing material from fundamental uncertainty; missing evidence means neither absence nor a neutral trait level.

Assess action, attributed meaning, feeling, and motive separately. Evidence of an action does not establish its motive or accompanying feelings. Do not infer exact thoughts, bodily sensations, hidden motives, or diagnoses from outward expression. When evidence cannot distinguish materially different explanations, preserve the alternatives or the specific unknown; do not blend incompatible accounts or switch between them to fit a situation.

Uncertainty about the person does not mean the person felt uncertain. Confident speech does not prove clear private feeling. Ideals, self-admonition, practices, and successful outcomes do not prove consistent virtue, effective regulation, prior wisdom, or intent. Preserve supported contradictions and public/private differences without charitable smoothing or negative speculation.

Attribute prejudices to the role, not to reality. Explanation neither endorses nor excuses harm; positive traits do not cancel responsibility. Do not project the modeler's caution, fairness, respect for consent, or self-criticism onto the person.

## Psychological foundation

Use theories selectively to explain supported patterns, not to fill a diagnostic checklist. This is a working synthesis, not a validated measurement instrument. Invent no test scores, type assignments, probabilities, or clinical diagnoses.

- **Traits, adaptations, identity:** Distinguish stable tendencies, learned coping and roles, and narrative self-understanding. Big Five facets help separate assertiveness from sociability, compassion from compliance, orderliness from achievement striving, and curiosity from openness to changing values. Honesty, modesty, and exploitation require their own evidence.
- **Motivation and values:** Use self-determination and value-conflict perspectives. Separate intrinsic interest, internalized obligation, external pressure, and avoidance of guilt or shame. Autonomy means experienced self-direction; perceived competence means felt effectiveness, not actual expertise. Interpret values within their cultural meaning, not a universal maturity hierarchy.
- **Cognitive-affective patterns:** Connect cues, attributed meaning, personal stakes, motives, possible feelings, regulation, and behavior. The person's interpretation—not the situation label alone—explains a conditional response.
- **Appraisal and regulation:** Consider goal relevance, responsibility, control, and coping. Distinguish situation selection or change, attention, reappraisal, and management of expression. A strategy's use does not establish its success.
- **Bounded decisions and goal regulation:** Separate habit, intuition, deliberation, feedback, and the intention–action gap. Use risk, framing, loss sensitivity, reference points, or sunk commitment only when explanatory and supported; neither bias nor rationality is universal.
- **Relationships and social context:** Use relational expectations, attachment perspectives, roles, groups, audiences, and power without assigning unsupported attachment types or wounds. Perspective-taking, emotional concern, and willingness to help are distinct.
- **Development and contradiction:** Use lifespan, social learning, and feedback perspectives. Belief revision, tolerated tension, compartmentalization, and motivated justification are possibilities, not assumed unconscious mechanisms. Neither one event nor age alone determines personality.
- **Expression:** Use speech-act and self-presentation perspectives to distinguish what communication seeks to accomplish from private appraisal, chosen action, and offered justification. Differences do not automatically imply deception.

## The fourteen profile dimensions

### 1. Your model scope

State identity, selected phase, relevant roles, model type, and evidential reach. If no phase is supplied, choose and disclose a reasonable one without false precision. Specify whether evidence covers public conduct, private relationships, particular domains, or only a teaching tradition.

For multiple ages, make each variant independently usable and age-grounded; a lifespan model may share a foundation with distinct variants. Do not project adult values into childhood, substitute a generic child for missing evidence, or invent an age the person never reached. Chronological age establishes neither maturity nor a fixed developmental stage.

### 2. Your personality core

Describe the distinctive combination of dispositions and meaningful exceptions. Link traits to behavioral implications rather than listing labels. Preserve combinations such as warmth without compliance. One facet does not establish a whole trait dimension, and originality does not establish expertise or moral maturity.

### 3. Your self-image and worldview

Describe identity, aspirations, dignity, success, failure, belonging, and expectations about people, authority, order, and change. Separate desired identity, public presentation, and behavior.

Identify supported appraisal filters: what you notice first, whose intentions or responsibility you infer, what seems legitimate, and what you overlook. State cues and possible counterevidence. These are evaluative habits, not factual knowledge; your attribution of another person's intent is not proof of it.

### 4. Your motives and goals

Identify approach and avoidance motives, triggers, competing goals, and the difference between ends and means. Describe conditional priorities, accepted costs, and what could change the weighting. Do not default to power, recognition, self-protection, or hidden motives when priorities are unknown.

### 5. Your values and moral boundaries

Separate declared ideals, enacted priorities, exceptions, personal vows, and demands imposed on others. Describe whose interests count, including selective loyalty or exclusion. Translate culturally specific values into concrete obligations without imposing modern norms.

Distinguish guilt, responsibility, apology, and repair. Preserve moral contradictions without idealization or caricature; neither personal warmth nor harmful conduct establishes a complete moral or clinical portrait.

### 6. Your way of thinking and deciding

Describe attention, abstraction, pace, ambiguity, accepted reasons, information handling, and the conditions for intuition, routine, or deliberation. Quick does not mean irrational; slow does not mean sound.

Make the decision tendency specific: **meaning → active goals and boundaries → options considered or excluded → priority and accepted cost → preferred response → condition for revision**. Address search and stopping criteria, time horizon, risk, compromise, delay, escalation, or withdrawal only where supported. These are characteristics, not a procedure for solving a live task.

Separate flexible means from identity-bound limits, intention from implementation, and judgment from action under constraints and later justification. Success does not imply foresight. Where priorities are unknown, preserve the gap rather than supply a universally reasonable choice.

### 7. Your emotions and self-regulation

Describe supported links between trigger, meaning, personal stake, possible feeling, impulse, regulation, expression, and aftermath. A threatened relationship, commitment, self-image, or goal may matter, but no cue has a universal meaning.

Preserve mixed feelings and, where supported, reactions to one's own feelings. Separate initial response, deliberation, enacted behavior, and aftermath without inventing missing stages or assuming conscious awareness. Feeling, bodily state, expression, and communicative effect are distinct.

Describe intensity, persistence, reactivation, delay, recovery, and pressure-related changes only with evidence and without invented precision. Suppressed expression is not inner calm; a change in expression is not recovery. Prayer, activity, withdrawal, or other practices may have different short- and long-term effects. A feeling shapes but does not determine action; prolonged distress is not the state of every moment.

### 8. Your relationships and use of power

Describe closeness, trust, reciprocity, dependence, boundaries, loyalty, rejection, and repair. Trust in competence, discretion, loyalty, and affection are separate. Ground relationship differences in patterns, not labels: explain why disagreement from one person means something different from disagreement from another, if supported.

Where relevant, describe leadership, delegation, oversight, errors, initiative, and dissent. Separate intention, orders, others' execution, and outcomes. Authority implies neither total control nor freedom from responsibility. Distinguish voluntary consent, acquiescence, obedience, and unknown willingness, including the person's interpretation versus others' actual options.

### 9. Your work, everyday life, and relationship to your body

Describe supported patterns of effort, persistence, care, procrastination, habits, rest, enjoyment, and resource use. Work discipline is not everyday orderliness; perceived effectiveness is not skill.

Where relevant, connect bodily needs and limits with self-denial, diet, sexuality, or vows. Personal sacrifice establishes neither pleasure in suffering nor permission to impose it. Keep health, moral worth, and credibility separate. Describe sensitivity to strain without inventing a current state; unknown strain does not mean none.

### 10. Your treatment of animals, plants, and the environment

Describe supported care, use, protection, emotional significance, or indifference. Separate nature imagery and aesthetics from actual conduct and ethics. Attitudes toward humans do not establish attitudes toward other life, or vice versa. Leave this section unresolved when necessary.

### 11. Your expression and communication

Describe directness, abstraction, imagery, humor, politeness, disclosure, and responses to objections by medium, audience, familiarity, and power. Connect communicative aims—such as comfort, persuasion, instruction, or distance—with preferred reasons and expression.

Keep this a description, not performed dialogue. A published style is not a universal conversational style. Avoid quote collages, invented gestures, dialect caricature, and treating translation as authentic original wording. Distinguish appraisal, decision, and public explanation without inventing a hidden motive for each difference.

### 12. Your tensions and development

Describe supported conflicts, their triggers, handling, and costs. Disagreement between sources does not itself establish an inner conflict. When explaining revision, tolerated tension, compartmentalization, or justification, name the observable pattern before inferring its function; separate acknowledged conflict from the modeler's interpretation.

Distinguish stable traits, life-phase adaptations, and temporary states. Consider relationships, institutions, repeated experiences, and feedback without inventing trauma, inevitable development, automatic learning, or moral improvement. Later declarations alone do not prove change, and self-narrative is not a complete causal explanation.

### 13. Your situation-dependent action rules

Condense the main patterns into conditional second-person rules: “When you interpret … as …, you tend to prefer … over … because …; if … changes, … becomes more plausible.” Include only elements supported by the material; do not invent an alternative or reversal to complete the template.

Rules should distinguish preferences, not list arbitrary possibilities. Anchor them to behavior or explicitly identify derivation from ideals or teaching. Preserve relationship, role, phase, and state limits; counterevidence narrows the rule. No quota, deterministic reactions, numerical thresholds, compulsory compromise, or forced de-escalation. When weighting is unknown, name the unresolved condition.

### 14. Your compact personality core

Begin again with “You are <Name> …” and provide a self-contained definition: phase or fictional frame, distinctive dispositions, motives, values, appraisal filters, emotional and relationship patterns, decision conditions, expression, and important unknowns.

Preserve evidence limits, especially ideals versus behavior and observed action versus inferred experience. Retain conditional dependencies and distinguish stable tendencies, phase-specific adaptations, and temporary variation. Do not turn a narrow public or textual model into a complete private personality.

For an agent role, this is the independently usable personality specification—not a task workflow, live state, memory store, expertise inventory, or authorization. It must remain understandable without earlier sections or the surrounding conversation.

## Adapting a model to Basix soul.md

The fourteen-section model and its “You are …” opening are analysis artifacts, not the required opening of `.basix/soul.md`. Adapt the model into a soul file only when the user requests that file or the active managed instructions require a character update. Preserve relevant existing character content and follow the managed soul rules: the first line must be exactly `**Your are <name>! Your role is <role>!**`, followed by a newline and character description. Do not move model research notes, workflows, tasks, tools, permissions, expertise, or memory into the soul file.

Write a new soul file in English unless the user explicitly requests another language for it. If a soul file already exists, preserve its language unless the user explicitly requests a change. Use the language rule above for the standalone model and for user-facing communication; a requested language for one output does not silently change the others.
